# Australian CPI expenditure class guide

This guide describes all **87 expenditure classes**, grouped into the **11 CPI groups**. Each entry gives a plain-English explanation and a practical reason why a single price or bill may not represent the entire category.

The class names follow the ABS classification. The examples are illustrative, not complete technical definitions or claims about every item currently sampled. Descriptions are deliberately simplified; for exact inclusions, exclusions and compilation rules, use the [ABS price collection chapter](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/detailed-methodology-information/concepts-sources-methods/consumer-price-index-concepts-sources-and-methods/2025/price-collection). The [coverage and classifications chapter](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/detailed-methodology-information/concepts-sources-methods/consumer-price-index-concepts-sources-and-methods/2025/coverage-and-classifications) explains how classes fit into the CPI.

No class weights, current inflation figures, forecasts or research results are included. The numbering below is for reading convenience, not an ABS classification code.

## Food and non-alcoholic beverages

### 1. Bread

Bread bought for home consumption, including loaves and rolls. A smaller loaf at the same shelf price is not the same price per amount of bread.

### 2. Cakes and biscuits

Cakes, biscuits and similar baked treats. Comparing an unchanged package or serving helps separate price changes from a switch to a different product.

### 3. Breakfast cereals

Breakfast cereal products such as oats and muesli. Box size and recipe differences matter when interpreting a shelf-price comparison.

### 4. Other cereal products

Grain-based staples outside the separate bread and breakfast-cereal classes, such as rice and pasta. Compare equivalent quantities and product types rather than unlike packages.

### 5. Beef and veal

Beef and veal purchased as food. The cut, preparation and quality can make two prices very different even when both products come from the same animal.

### 6. Pork

Pork products, including familiar purchases such as pork cuts, bacon and ham. A price comparison should distinguish the product and its preparation rather than combine everything labelled pork.

### 7. Lamb and goat

Meat from sheep and goats. Different cuts and pack sizes make like-for-like comparisons more informative than a simple average of displayed prices.

### 8. Poultry

Poultry meat, such as chicken or turkey. Whole birds and prepared cuts are different products, so a changing mix of purchases can change average spending without a comparable price change.

### 9. Other meats

Meat products not captured by the more specific meat classes, including products such as sausages and salami. This is a classification category, not a measure of all meat combined.

### 10. Fish and other seafood

Fish and seafood purchases. Species, preparation and whether a product is fresh, frozen or preserved can all affect the comparison.

### 11. Milk

Milk products, with different forms and varieties represented in the category. Container volume and product type matter: a cheaper carton is not necessarily cheaper per litre.

### 12. Cheese

Cheese purchases. Variety, maturity, brand and pack size can differ substantially, so not every change in the average price of purchases represents inflation.

### 13. Ice cream and other dairy products

Dairy foods such as yoghurt, cream and ice cream that sit outside the separate milk and cheese classes. Product size and composition are useful context for price changes.

### 14. Fruit

Fruit purchases, rather than only one fruit or one fresh-produce stall. Seasonal availability and the mix of varieties can make an individual shopping trip unrepresentative of the wider class.

### 15. Vegetables

Vegetable purchases across different products and forms. A price spike in one crop need not describe all vegetables, and comparisons should allow for quantity and quality.

### 16. Eggs

Eggs bought for consumption. Carton count, egg size and production type distinguish products and should not be mistaken for price changes in an otherwise identical purchase.

### 17. Jams, honey and spreads

Spreads such as jam, honey and savoury dips. Jar size and ingredients help explain why superficially similar products can have different prices.

### 18. Food additives and condiments

Ingredients used to season or sweeten food, including sauces, spices and sugar. The category is broader than any single condiment, and small packages can have high unit prices.

### 19. Oils and fats

Cooking oils and edible fats, including butter and margarine. Container size and the type of oil or fat are important to a meaningful comparison.

### 20. Snacks and confectionery

Snack foods and sweets, such as chips and chocolate. Multipacks, serving sizes and promotional offers can make the displayed price difficult to compare directly.

### 21. Other food products n.e.c.

A residual category for food products not elsewhere classified, such as prepared meals and baby food. The abbreviation 'n.e.c.' means 'not elsewhere classified'; it does not mean unmeasured.

### 22. Coffee, tea and cocoa

Coffee, tea and cocoa products purchased as groceries. A packet of coffee is a different purchase from a drink served in a cafe, where service is part of the price.

### 23. Waters, soft drinks and juices

Packaged waters, soft drinks and juices. Bottle size, multipack quantity and product formulation can change the value received even when the displayed price stays the same.

### 24. Restaurant meals

Meals supplied with restaurant-style table service. The purchase includes preparation and service, not just the ingredients that a household could buy separately.

### 25. Take away and fast foods

Ready-to-eat takeaway, delivered and fast-food meals. A meal can belong here even when it is eaten at tables provided by the outlet rather than taken home.

## Alcohol and tobacco

### 26. Spirits

Spirits and related alcoholic drinks. Bottle volume, strength and whether the drink is bought in a shop or served at a venue affect what is being compared.

### 27. Wine

Wine and related drinks in this classification. A restaurant glass and a retail bottle are different purchases; vintage, package size and brand also matter.

### 28. Beer

Beer purchases, including different styles and packaging. A change in the mix of cans, bottles and drinks served at venues is not automatically a like-for-like price change.

### 29. Tobacco

Tobacco products within the official CPI's scope. Pack contents and taxes can affect prices. This classification is descriptive and is not an endorsement of tobacco use.

## Clothing and footwear

### 30. Garments for men

Men's clothing for uses such as work, leisure and sleep. Changing fabrics, construction and seasonal ranges can complicate comparisons between old and replacement items.

### 31. Garments for women

Women's clothing across everyday and other uses. A lower average price during a sale may reflect genuine discounts, a different selection of garments, or both.

### 32. Garments for infants and children

Clothing for babies and children. Size, material and garment type matter when comparing prices; a change in what a growing child needs is not itself inflation.

### 33. Footwear for men

Men's footwear for ordinary use. Quality, materials and intended use distinguish products, so an inexpensive casual shoe is not a direct replacement for every other shoe.

### 34. Footwear for women

Women's footwear for ordinary use. Comparisons are clearer when they keep the type and quality of shoe consistent rather than compare changing fashion ranges indiscriminately.

### 35. Footwear for infants and children

Footwear for babies and children, including everyday and school use. A household buying a different size or type as a child grows may see spending change independently of prices.

### 36. Accessories

Personal accessories such as jewellery, bags and conventional watches. Materials and features vary widely, making the specification of the item important.

### 37. Cleaning, repair and hire of clothing and footwear

Services that clean, repair or provide clothing and footwear for hire. The service being purchased, rather than the replacement cost of the garment or shoe, is the relevant comparison.

## Housing

### 38. Rents

Rent paid for a household's main residence. Advertised rents for available properties describe a different set of observations from rents paid across existing tenancies.

### 39. New dwelling purchase by owner-occupiers

The dwelling component of new owner-occupied housing, excluding land. It is not an index of established-home sale prices or mortgage repayments. Major improvements and certain fixed appliances also belong to this class.

### 40. Maintenance and repair of the dwelling

Materials and services for maintaining or repairing a home. The size and nature of the job matter; routine repairs should not be confused with purchasing a new dwelling.

### 41. Property rates and charges

Property-related rates and charges imposed by state or local government. Water and sewerage have their own class, so a council bill can contain items belonging to different categories.

### 42. Water and sewerage

Household water-supply and sewerage charges. Bills may combine service charges and usage charges, so spending can rise because of higher use as well as a changed tariff.

### 43. Electricity

Household electricity charges. The price paid can depend on supply charges, usage rates, plans and eligible rebates; an increase in electricity consumption is a separate issue.

### 44. Gas and other household fuels

Household gas and other fuels used at home, such as firewood. Different fuels and supply arrangements should not be treated as interchangeable price quotes.

## Furnishings, household equipment and services

### 45. Furniture

Furniture for the home, such as tables, chairs and beds. Materials, dimensions and construction can change between products, so a replacement range may need more than a sticker-price comparison.

### 46. Carpets and other floor coverings

Carpets and other materials used to cover floors. Area, material and installation arrangements affect the meaning of a quoted price.

### 47. Household textiles

Textile items used around the home, such as towels, bed linen and curtains. Size, fabric and the number of items in a set affect comparability.

### 48. Major household appliances

Large household appliances, such as refrigerators and washing machines. Capacity, efficiency and features can change between models; a more expensive replacement is not necessarily all price inflation.

### 49. Small electric household appliances

Smaller electrical appliances used in the home, such as kettles and toasters. Compare the product's function and features as well as its price.

### 50. Glassware, tableware and household utensils

Items used to prepare, serve and consume food, including cookware and crockery. A set containing more pieces is not directly comparable with a smaller set.

### 51. Tools and equipment for house and garden

Tools and equipment used for household and garden tasks. Size, durability and whether power tools include batteries or accessories can affect the apparent price.

### 52. Cleaning and maintenance products

Consumable products used to clean and maintain the home. Pack volume, concentration and the number of uses can be more informative than the headline bottle price.

### 53. Personal care products

Products used for personal hygiene and grooming. This class concerns goods, while paid grooming services have their own class; pack size and formulation can also change.

### 54. Other non-durable household products

Short-lived household supplies not assigned to the other product classes, such as tissues and other disposable items. The quantity in a pack is important when reading price changes.

### 55. Child care

Paid care for children. The fee a provider advertises may differ from what an eligible family pays after assistance; the type and amount of care also matter.

### 56. Hairdressing and personal grooming services

Paid hairdressing and personal grooming. Service length, inclusions and type can differ, so compare equivalent appointments rather than every advertised service price.

### 57. Other household services

Household services that do not sit in the more specific classes, such as domestic cleaning. The job's scope and frequency affect how a service quote should be interpreted.

## Health

### 58. Pharmaceutical products

Medicines purchased by households. Brand, dosage and pack quantity distinguish products, while subsidies and concessions can affect the amount paid by an eligible customer.

### 59. Therapeutic appliances and equipment

Health-related aids and equipment, such as spectacles and hearing aids. Features, fitting and associated services can make a simple comparison of advertised prices misleading.

### 60. Medical and hospital services

Medical and hospital care and related health-insurance costs. The household's out-of-pocket price can differ from a provider's full charge because of rebates or insurance arrangements.

### 61. Dental services

Dental treatment and related services. A check-up, filling and more complex procedure are different purchases; treatment scope and any rebate affect the cost to the household.

## Transport

### 62. Motor vehicles

Motor vehicles acquired by households. Vehicle specifications and equipment matter, and the purchase price is distinct from loan repayments or the cost of running the vehicle.

### 63. Spare parts and accessories for motor vehicles

Vehicle parts and accessories, such as replacement tyres and batteries. The item must suit the vehicle, and specifications or included fitting can change the meaning of a quote.

### 64. Automotive fuel

Fuel used in motor vehicles. Fuel grade, outlet, location and purchase date can all affect the price; a wholesale price is not the same as the amount paid at the pump.

### 65. Maintenance and repair of motor vehicles

Services that maintain and repair vehicles. Labour, parts and the scope of the work can differ, so two workshop bills may not represent the same service.

### 66. Other services in respect of motor vehicles

Other vehicle-related services and charges, including registration, parking and road tolls. These are separate from buying a vehicle, buying fuel or paying for repairs.

### 67. Urban transport fares

Fares for urban passenger transport. Journey length, ticket type and concession eligibility affect the fare paid, so a headline adult fare does not describe every passenger.

## Communication

### 68. Postal services

Postal services used by households. Weight, delivery speed, destination and service type distinguish one postage charge from another.

### 69. Telecommunication equipment and services

Communication devices and services, including mobile and internet services. More data or different inclusions at the same subscription price can change the service being purchased.

## Recreation and culture

### 70. Audio, visual and computing equipment

Equipment used for computing and audio or visual entertainment. New models may change performance, storage or features, so sticker-price changes alone do not capture a constant-quality comparison.

### 71. Audio, visual and computing media and services

Media and services used for audio, visual or computing activities. Subscription tiers, access rights and bundled content can change even when a recurring payment does not.

### 72. Books

Books bought by households. Format, edition and title affect the product, so comparing the average prices of different selections of books can be misleading.

### 73. Newspapers, magazines and stationery

Newspapers, magazines and stationery. A subscription, a single issue and a stationery multipack are different purchases and should be understood on their own terms.

### 74. Domestic holiday travel and accommodation

Holiday travel and accommodation within Australia. Destination, season, length of stay and booking conditions can all affect prices; the booking date and travel date are not the same thing.

### 75. International holiday travel and accommodation

Overseas holiday travel and accommodation. Trip details and currency movements can affect prices, but a single airfare or exchange rate does not describe the whole class.

### 76. Equipment for sports, camping and open-air recreation

Goods used for sport, camping and outdoor recreation. Compare equivalent equipment and distinguish buying an item from paying to participate in an activity.

### 77. Games, toys and hobbies

Toys, games and goods used for hobbies. Product versions, included pieces and accessories can differ, making a cheaper package an imperfect comparison with a larger one.

### 78. Pets and related products

Pets and goods used to care for them, such as pet food. This is separate from veterinary and other paid pet services; pack size and product type remain important.

### 79. Veterinary and other services for pets

Veterinary treatment and other services for pets. The animal, treatment and services included can vary, so a single quoted consultation fee does not describe every pet-care bill.

### 80. Sports participation

Fees for taking part in sport and exercise, such as memberships or activity charges. Contract length and access to facilities can differ between apparently similar offers.

### 81. Other recreational, sporting and cultural services

Recreational and cultural services outside the other classes, including admissions to events or attractions. Ticket category, venue and the service offered affect what is being priced.

## Education

### 82. Preschool and primary education

Preschool and primary-school education services. This category is separate from secondary and tertiary education; tuition, compulsory charges and eligible assistance can affect what households pay.

### 83. Secondary education

Secondary-school education services. Different schools and fee inclusions make a single advertised fee an incomplete picture of the class.

### 84. Tertiary education

Education beyond secondary school, including higher education and vocational study. Course type, fee arrangements and eligibility for assistance affect the price faced by a student.

## Insurance and financial services

### 85. Insurance

Household insurance such as home and motor cover. Cover limits, excesses and the insured circumstances must be comparable; health insurance is treated within medical and hospital services.

### 86. Deposit and loan facilities (direct charges)

Direct fees for banking and lending services, such as account or transaction charges. This class is about service fees, not the amount borrowed or the interest paid on a loan.

### 87. Other financial services

Financial and related transaction services outside direct banking fees and insurance, including property-transfer services and charges. These service costs are not the same as the price of the asset being transferred.
